_**Correction**: Your chances of building a better \[product] than \[company]
are miniscule, and the bigger the company is, the smaller your chances are._

I see a lot of startup ideas online, some that are good that I would genuinely
pay for, some that don't quite fit me but I can see the market, and some (a lot)
that are just plain stupid. Let's take a look at some ideas that I think are stupid,
why I think so, and if they could be saved with some tweaking.

## Underestimating your competitor

One mistake I come across often is people underestimating the mass of the
monster they're up against. This is the case for ideas like:

> \[Main product of a successful company], but better

I see a lot of people pitching this, like they have some feature or aspect
that would completely disrupt the market and turn everyone to their product.
The biggest problem with these ideas is overestimating the novelty of their idea,
and conversely, underestimating the creative energy spent within the company on
that product. What they need to realize is that the company has (probably) also
considered that idea. There are teams of people whose entire job is to optimize
every miniscule part of the product. And they live off of that.

People feed families, buy houses, save to retire, people are hired, fired, and
promoted based on their ability to think about and optimize parts of the product
you haven't even thought about yet. Brilliant people working throughout the day
to put a roof over their heads and food in their stomachs, whose entire goal is
to make that product better. Internal competitions with real prizes are held to
find new ways to improve the product. Promotions are routinely given out for
taking one of those optimizations from conception to completion.

_And you think you're gonna **beat** them?_

You're severely underestimating your opponent. Chances are, your idea has been
pitched somewhere within the company (probably a few times), by one of their 1000+
employees looking for a personal gain, and it's either in development right now
or (most likely) got shot down because market research or other barriers have concluded
that there's no money in it, or at least not enough to warrant any sort of priority.
Especially if there's a way to make it work within the existing product, even if
it's not completely native.

The same reasoning also applies to ideas like:

> Like \[product] but with \[feature]

And think about it: let's say you go through with it. Let's say you build the product,
attract some users, and get a pretty good thing going. Guess what, if the company
you're competing against (you know, the company with way more users than you, who
you're probably stealing users from) sees that success, there is absolutely nothing
stopping them from building the one (or two, or however many) feature your product
has as its entire selling point, and integrating it for free with their app, making
your whole business irrelevant. If this is your business idea, it better not be
long-term. Your best bet is to find a niche that has a market, hyper-optimize for
that, then look for a buyer as soon as you gain real traction. One thing to note:
Your "moat" scales proportionately with the amount of features you have, so if you're
really revamping something, you might have a solid idea.

**Examples**:

- Instagram stories and disappearing messages made Snapchat basically irrelevant.
- Outlook bought Sunrise calendar, integrated it, and sunset it.
- When twitter bought and integrated periscope, making "Livestream to twitter followers"
  apps unprofitable.

That third example is kind of different because it was a new idea, the company
that introduced it (Meerkat) had a user-base and everything, but Twitter pulled
the rug from underneath them. This leads to another mistake:

## Building a house on someone else's land

This is one you might overlook, or you might think you're not doing, but this is
surprisingly common, especially nowadays. Building a house on someone else's land
means your entire product is reliant on another product.

**Examples**:

- Tile relied on multiple devices creating a mesh network; Samsung & Apple, the
  two biggest smartphone manufacturers, implemented their own solution and made
  Tile irrelevant immediately.
- When's the last time you heard of Dropbox? Thank Apple, Google, and Microsoft
  for that.
- AI photo/video generator websites - OpenAI and Google made quick work of those
  companies when they released their own photo/video generators.

That third example is something to seriously look out for. Google, Anthropic, or
OpenAI can steal your entire business if they think there's real money in it and
they can do it better. Remember these companies aren't just gonna let you exist.
They're not content with being the biggest fish in the pond, they want to be the
only fish so they can eat all the food.

These types of ideas, like the other ones, are basically early-exist ideas; if you're
dead-set on building this out, aim for a quick exist, because you don't have a long-term
product.

## So what's a good idea?

You're gonna have to look. Most business ideas that actually have some potential
are solving a problem that the founder encountered themselves, looked for a solution,
and found none. If you have encountered a problem and solved it yourself, and then
found others with the same problem willing to pay for it, you might have a business.
But there's a trap here too, and that's building a product for a customer base of
one (yourself). You solve that with market research and validation, but people like
to skip that crucial step and are confused when their product fails to even get off
the ground.
