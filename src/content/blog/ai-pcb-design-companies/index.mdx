When I was building Electrocaddy, while I didn't know what direction I wanted to
take it, I thought I was breaking new ground, creating something nobody else had
done before, or at least done right. I got to building thinking I was one of the
pioneers in the field, that I would have some widespread adoption because
nobody's seen this before and my implementation is gonna be "right", a whole
bunch of starry-eyed visions that ignored the underlying question, which was "is
anyone else doing this? What is the niche I can actually target?"

I had failed to do the most important thing before starting any project that you
plan to commercialize: market research. I just got started and actually spent
$1,000 on windsurf credits building out my solution, which, in hindsight, was
very rudimentary. I had barely scratched the surface of modeling datasheets and
component specs, what I did model was processed and extracted poorly, and the
small amount of verification/circuit knowledge I had relied on the AI getting a
massive JSON file and reasoning about that, which threw any reliability and
consistency out the window.

So, when starting my second implementation, I decided to do some research and
see what was out there, especially since I had come across a few companies doing
this & sponsoring videos on youtube. And yeah, there are... a lot. So it begs
the question: how come none of these have taken off? Let's look into each
company, the product(s) they offer, what they do well & what they suck at, along
with any sentiment from online reviews or comments.

<CompanyList />

So there we have it, 13 companies who have tried/are trying to solve the same
problem, in different ways. We can see how some companies try to move the whole
ecosystem onto their platform, like Protoflow and JITX, some try to integrate
with existing platforms, like BQR, Cadstrom, Circuit Mind, and others, and some
are going in-between (like quilter, where you design in ECAD, then upload to
their platform). Some of them do simulations, some do schematic generation, some
do PCB layout, some do all of it, but all of them have one thing in common: they
haven't really taken off. (BQR might have some solid adoption) Why is that?

Well, another thing all of these have in common is a severely lacking marketing
campaign or department or anything like that. Nobody's talking about these guys!
I had to search pretty deep to find some of these players, and there are
probably some more out there that I'm missing. Why do these companies suck at
marketing? Is it a deliberate purpose, putting as much money into the quality of
the product as possible? Could be, although companies that have raised a solid
bit of money can't really use this as an excuse.

Online sentiment is that all of these suck. They're inaccurate, you always have
to fix things, they're seen more as jokes or entertainment, time-killing
curiosity sinks rather than real tools. That would indicate that the quality of
the product is, in fact, bad, and that the companies are right to be spending as
much resources as possible trying to improve quality. One could also say that
online forums are a harsh crowd, especially with AI fatigue settling in.

I thought I would do this research and come out with a niche like "nobody has
done THIS." No, multiple companies have done that. Maybe not in the way I was
thinking, but definitely in a way. So why hasn't a solution been adopted? Or,
why hasn't enough demand been created where a business that could have a chance
at getting it right, hasn't done it yet? Compare this to software, where any
idea you come up with is probably being developed right now by some guy willing
to shovel tokens into the AI furnace.

My immediate answer is that electrical engineering has higher stakes, therefore
there are more processes involved, therefore more inertia when making decisions
like this. This does track, especially if you look at other disciplines where
mistakes are costly, like mechanical engineering. I don't see AI disheveling
that community any time soon, and Autodesk is trying. There is also the idea
that software can be patched & updated while electronics usually can't, and
circuit boards definitely can't. So a mistake in a board that goes into
production costs a whole lot more than a mistake in software. Engineers know
that and show it in their processes and decisions. Even firmware developers
aren't really playing around here.

So where does that leave an aspirational founder? What does that tell someone
who wants to start a business, build a product, and revolutionize the game? For
me, it tells me that the demand probably isn't there. Think about who the target
buyer is. People who don't know enough about electrical engineering/designing
PCBs probably aren't gonna come up with a business idea that requires a custom
PCB, because they don't have the first clue on how to do that. Anyone who does
know enough to design a board isn't gonna wanna use it, because they would have
to check everything over anwyays.

All in all, this was a very interesting exercise in market research. Finding out
how other teams are solving the same problem I'm looking to solve, and where
they fell short in ways I could possibly improve on, was incredibly
informational, and pretty fun. Just sucks that I couldn't get any definitive
answers though.

Maybe the market research isn't supposed to teach the lesson, maybe the market
research is the lesson.
